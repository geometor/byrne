Book II Notes — Half-Filled Woodblocks & Q(phi) Areas

Inspection of Pickering pp. 56–57 (Leaves 90–91) shows a striking Chiswick Press graphic device: instead of printing the two diagonal sub-squares in solid ink, Byrne fills only the upper-left triangle of each square while outlining the boundary (, ). Because Euclid first proves in II.4 that the two sub-triangles cut by the diagonal are isosceles ([I.6]) before concluding that the four-sided figures are squares, half-filling makes both the constituent triangle and the enclosing square simultaneously legible in a single woodblock impression.

Book III Notes — Multi-Case Plates & Rational Circle Models

In Book III, Euclid frequently distinguishes geometric configurations according to whether the circle’s center lies on, within, or outside an inscribed angle (III.20, p. 102) or whether a secant passes through or aside from the center (III.36, p. 121). On these pages, Whittingham sets FIGURE I and FIGURE II vertically on the same quarto leaf, reusing the color semantics across both cases.

Book IV Notes — Five-Edge Pentagon Overflow & Cyclotomic Radicals

Proposition IV.11 (Pickering p. 137, Leaf 171) poses a sharp combinatorial test for Oliver Byrne: a regular pentagon has five distinct perimeter chords, yet the Chiswick Press palette has only four inks (red, blue, yellow, black), and the inscribed Golden Triangle already occupies solid yellow ()!

Book V Notes — Bridge to geometor/codes & Dedekind Cuts

In the GEOMETOR ecosystem, geometor/codes investigates discrete and symbolic encodings of ratio, continued fractions, Ostrowski/Sturmian words, and Eudoxian anthyphairesis. Oliver Byrne's Book V notation (Pickering pp. 153–212) is the 1847 typographic ancestor of geometor/codes, turning Book V into an ordered $\mathbb{N}^+$-semimodule calculus isomorphic to Dedekind’s 1872 cut construction of $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$.

Book VI Notes — Application of Areas & Conic Ancestors

As Proclus (Friedlein, geometor/proclus) and Heath (1908) explain, Greek application of areas (parabole, elleipsis, hyperbole) is the direct geometric ancestor of Apollonius's conic sections (geometor/apollonius). In VI.30, applying a rectangle of area $1$ to a unit segment with square excess ($\phi^{-1}(1 + \phi^{-1}) = 1$) solves $x(1 + x) = 1 \implies x = \phi^{-1} = \frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}$ in $\mathbb{Q}(\phi)$.
